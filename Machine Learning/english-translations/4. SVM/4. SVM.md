## 1. About SVM

### 1.1 A Fairy Tale About SVM

Support Vector Machine (SVM) is one of the most prominent supervised learning methods. There is a story about angels and devils.

The devil placed balls of two colors on a table and asked the angel to separate them with a stick. The angel did it easily. The devil added more balls. As the number grew, some balls could no longer be separated by the original stick.

![](http://wx4.sinaimg.cn/mw690/00630Defgy1g4vn6ow8vtj30i40ddgpt.jpg)

SVM finds the best placement for the stick so that balls on both sides stay sufficiently far from it. Even if the devil keeps adding balls, the stick still separates the two classes well.

With linear separation solved, the devil gave a new challenge: the balls could not be separated by any stick.

![](http://wx4.sinaimg.cn/mw690/00630Defgy1g4vn9xl3pcj30iy0dswgr.jpg)

The angel had powers: he made the balls fly into the air and inserted a sheet of paper between the two classes. From the devil's view, the balls appeared perfectly separated by a curve.

![](http://wx2.sinaimg.cn/mw690/00630Defgy1g4vnbaltf7j30mo0ar77n.jpg)

Scientists later called the balls "data," the stick "decision boundary," the process of finding the maximum margin "optimization," the power to lift balls "kernel mapping," and the sheet in the air "classification hyperplane." That is the fairy tale of SVM.

### 1.2 Understanding SVM: Level 1

SVM is a binary classifier. Its basic model is a linear classifier that maximizes the margin in feature space. The learning strategy is margin maximization, which can be cast as a convex quadratic programming problem.

**Linear classifier**: Given data points from two classes, find a linear classifier to separate them. With x as data points and y as labels (1 or -1), the goal is to find a hyperplane w^Tx + b = 0 in n-dimensional space. (T denotes transpose.)

This hyperplane can be represented by f(x) = w^Tx + b. When f(x)=0, x is on the hyperplane; f(x)>0 corresponds to y=1, f(x)<0 to y=-1.

#### 1.2.1 Functional Margin and Geometric Margin

Given hyperplane w*x+b=0, |w*x+b| measures how far x is from the hyperplane. The sign of (y*(w*x+b)) indicates correctness. This leads to **functional margin**: γ = y(w^Tx+b) = yf(x).

The minimum functional margin over all samples in T defines the functional margin of the hyperplane.

Scaling w and b (e.g., 2w, 2b) scales the functional margin but leaves the hyperplane unchanged. So functional margin alone is insufficient.

**Geometric margin**

We constrain the normal vector w to define the true distance—**geometric margin**. For a point x with projection x0 on the hyperplane, w is perpendicular to the hyperplane, and γ is the distance from x to the hyperplane.

Geometric margin: ![](https://latex.codecogs.com/gif.latex?\gamma^{'}=\frac{\gamma}{||w||})

Geometric margin = functional margin / ||w||. The geometric margin is the intuitive distance; it is invariant to scaling of w and b.

#### 1.2.2 Maximum Margin Classifier

Larger margin implies higher classification confidence. We choose the hyperplane that maximizes this margin (half of the "Gap" in the figure).

Function margin is unsuitable for maximization because scaling w and b can make it arbitrarily large without changing the hyperplane. Geometric margin is invariant and is what we maximize.

The optimal hyperplane lies midway between the two margin boundaries. Support vectors lie on the margin and satisfy y(w^Tx+b)=1; other points satisfy y(w^Tx+b)>1.

#### 1.2.3 Maximum Margin Loss Function: Hinge Loss

SVM can be formulated as a quadratic program and solved via Lagrange multipliers and duality. Alternatively, we can use empirical risk minimization with **Hinge Loss**:

![](https://latex.codecogs.com/gif.latex?L_i=\sum_{j\neq_{}t_i}max(0,f(x_i,W)_j-(f(x_i,W)_{y_i}-\bigtriangleup)))

- For the i-th training sample xi
- f(xi,W) is the score vector under W
- f(xi,W)j is the score for class j

Interpretation: We want a safety margin. Delta is a safety threshold. Prediction errors beyond this threshold incur penalty. When the wrong score exceeds correct_score - delta, there is loss; otherwise loss is 0. Training iterates until the loss is minimized and the classification hyperplane is found.

### 1.3 Deep Dive into SVM: Level 2

#### 1.3.1 From Linear to Nonlinear

When data is not linearly separable, no such hyperplane exists. SVM handles nonlinear data by choosing a kernel κ(·,·) and mapping data to a higher-dimensional space where it becomes linearly separable.

**For linearly inseparable cases, SVM first computes in low dimensions, then uses the kernel to map to high-dimensional feature space, where it constructs the optimal separating hyperplane.** E.g., 2D data that cannot be separated may become separable in 3D.

Common kernels: **polynomial, Gaussian (RBF), linear.**

Key points about kernels:
1. Linear inseparable cases are often handled by mapping to high dimensions.
2. Direct high-dimensional computation can be prohibitively expensive.
3. The kernel implicitly computes in high dimensions while operating in low dimensions, avoiding the cost.

**Slack variables** can be used when there are outliers.

#### 1.3.2 Summary

SVM is essentially a classifier defined by w^T+b. To maximize margin we introduce 1/2||w||^2, then Lagrange multipliers, and solve for the dual variables a. Solving for w,b is equivalent to solving for a. A fast algorithm (SMO) is used for a. The kernel handles nonlinearity without explicit high-dimensional computation.

### 1.4 Applications of SVM

SVM is widely used in **text classification, image classification, biological sequence analysis, handwritten character recognition**, and many other domains.

## 2. Some Questions About SVM

1. Does there exist a set of parameters such that SVM training error is 0? **Yes.**
2. Does a classifier with 0 training error always exist? **Yes.**
3. Can SVM with slack variables achieve 0 training error? **Not necessarily.** With SMO, the objective changes; we no longer minimize training error.
4. **Why can kernel SVM classify nonlinear problems?** The kernel is an inner product that implicitly maps to high dimensions, where nonlinear problems become linear. The resulting hyperplane is linear in high dimensions but corresponds to nonlinear boundaries in low dimensions.
5. **How to choose the kernel?**
   - If #features ≈ #samples: use LR or linear kernel SVM.
   - If #features is small and #samples is moderate: use SVM with Gaussian kernel.
   - If #features is small and #samples is large: add features to approximate the first case.

## 3. Relationship and Differences Between LR and SVM

### 3.1 Similarities

- Both are linear classifiers; both seek an optimal hyperplane.
- Both are supervised learning algorithms.
- Both are discriminative models (they model P(y|x) or decision boundaries, not data generation).

### 3.2 Differences

- LR is parametric; SVM is nonparametric. Linear vs. RBF kernels correspond to linear vs. nonlinear separability.
- LR uses logistic loss; SVM uses hinge loss. Both emphasize points that matter for classification.
- SVM considers only support vectors; LR down-weights points far from the decision boundary.
- LR is simpler and scales well for large linear classification. SVM optimization is more complex, but in dual form classification depends only on distances to few support vectors, which simplifies computation with complex kernels.
- LR can do most of what SVM does (possibly with lower accuracy); some SVM capabilities are beyond LR.

## 4. Linear vs. Nonlinear Classifiers

Linearity vs. nonlinearity refers to the model parameters and input features. E.g., y=ax+ax^2 with input x is nonlinear; with inputs x and x^2 it is linear.

- **Linear classifiers** (LR, Naive Bayes, single-layer perceptron, linear regression): interpretable, lower computational cost; weaker fitting ability.
- **Nonlinear classifiers** (decision trees, RF, GBDT, multilayer perceptron): stronger fitting ability; prone to overfitting with limited data; higher cost; less interpretable.

**SVM can be either** (linear or Gaussian kernel).
