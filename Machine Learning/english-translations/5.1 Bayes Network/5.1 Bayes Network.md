## 1. Understanding Probabilistic Graphical Models

Probabilistic graphical models use graphs to represent probability dependencies among variables, combining probability theory and graph theory. They were developed by Turing Award winner Pearl.

In one word, probabilistic graphical models are "elegant." For a real problem, we want to uncover knowledge hidden in data. The model builds a graph: observed nodes for data, latent nodes for hidden knowledge, edges for relationships. **From this graph we obtain a probability distribution** that elegantly addresses the problem.

Nodes can be latent or observed; edges can be directed or undirected. From a probability perspective, nodes are random variables; edges represent dependence or correlation. **Directed edges indicate one-way dependence; undirected edges indicate mutual dependence.**

Probabilistic graphical models include **Bayesian Networks** (directed) and **Markov Networks** (undirected). Bayesian networks use directed acyclic graphs (DAGs); Markov networks use undirected graphs. Examples: Naive Bayes, Maximum Entropy, HMM, CRF, topic models—all widely used in ML.

## 2. Bayesian Networks in Detail

### 2.1 Frequentist View

For a long time, probability was seen as fixed: either 0 or 1—either it happens or it doesn’t. Probability might be unknown but was assumed to be a fixed constant. E.g., "A bag contains white and black balls; what is the probability of drawing white?" The answer was 1/2—you either get white or not. **θ stayed 1/2 regardless of observations**—it did not depend on data X.

This **frequentist** view dominated until Thomas Bayes appeared.

### 2.2 Bayesian View

Thomas Bayes (1702–1763) was little known in his time. He published "An essay towards solving a problem in the doctrine of chances." His key insight: the probability of drawing white is uncertain because it involves chance. For example, when a friend starts a business, you might estimate an 80% chance of success based on their character, methods, and persistence. This "not strictly 0 or 1" way of thinking is **Bayesian thinking**.

**Summary:**
- **Frequentist**: Parameter θ is a fixed unknown constant; sample X is random. Focus on the sample space and distributions of X.
- **Bayesian**: Parameter θ is a random variable; sample X is fixed. Focus on the distribution of θ.

To compute the distribution of θ, we need its unconditional distribution before observing X—the **prior distribution**. E.g., a ball thrown on a table lands uniformly at random—uniform prior. Priors often come from experience or historical data. **Posterior** π(θ|X) is the conditional distribution of θ given X. The value θ that maximizes π(θ|X) is the **maximum a posteriori (MAP)** estimate, analogous to **maximum likelihood (MLE)** in frequentist statistics.

Over time, more observations lead to better understanding of nature. Bayesian methods align with both everyday reasoning and how we learn from data.

### 2.4 Bayesian Network

A **Bayesian network** (Belief Network, directed acyclic graphical model) is a probabilistic graphical model proposed by Judea Pearl (1985). It models causal relationships with uncertainty. The topology is a DAG.

Nodes represent random variables {X1, X2, ..., Xn}—observed, latent, or parameters. Arrows connect causally related variables. An arrow from E to H means E is the "cause" (parent) and H is the "effect" (child), with weight P(H|E).

For any set of random variables, the joint probability factorizes as:

![](https://latex.codecogs.com/gif.latex?P(x_1,...,x_k)=P(x_k|x_1,...,x_{k-1})...P(x_2|x_1)P(x_1))

#### 2.4.1 Structures in Bayesian Networks

**1. head-to-head**

P(a,b,c) = P(a)*P(b)*P(c|a,b). When c is unknown, a and b are independent (blocked)—head-to-head conditional independence.

**2. tail-to-tail**

When c is unknown: P(a,b,c) = P(c)*P(a|c)*P(b|c); a and b are not independent.
When c is known: P(a,b|c) = P(a|c)*P(b|c); a and b are independent.

**3. head-to-tail**

When c is unknown: a and b are not independent.
When c is known: a and b are independent (blocked)—head-to-tail conditional independence.

The head-to-tail structure is a chain. Given xi, xi+1 is independent of x1,...,xi-1. The current state depends only on the previous state—a **Markov chain**.

#### 2.4.2 Factor Graphs

A factor graph is a bipartite graph obtained by factorizing a global function into a product of local functions. It has variable nodes and function nodes. Factor graphs support efficient inference (e.g., sum-product algorithm) for marginal distributions.

### 2.5 Naive Bayes

Naive Bayes is a classic ML algorithm and one of the few classification methods based on probability. It is simple and widely used (e.g., spam filtering). **Naive Bayes is a special case of Bayesian networks: no edges, all nodes independent.**

Why "naive"? **Two assumptions:**
- Each feature is conditionally independent of the others.
- All features are equally important.

**Why assume independence?**
1. Without it, estimating joint probabilities is infeasible with many features and values.
2. Without it, we need counts over the full feature space, which are often zero due to sparsity.

This strong assumption simplifies the model but can hurt accuracy when features are correlated.

**Pros**: Simple, easy to implement; low time and space cost.
**Cons**: Independence rarely holds; performance degrades when features are correlated or numerous. The "naive" means we naively assume independence; in practice it often works when correlations are weak.

## 3. Questions Based on Bayesian Methods

1. Explain prior probability, likelihood, and marginal likelihood in Naive Bayes?
   - **Prior probability**: Proportion of the class (e.g., binary) in the dataset—the best guess without further information.
   - **Likelihood**: Probability that an observation is classified as 1 given other variables. E.g., P("FREE" in spam).
   - **Marginal likelihood**: P("FREE" in any message).

## 4. Generative vs. Discriminative Models

- **Discriminative models** predict y via P(y|x) or direct mapping: Linear Regression, Logistic Regression, SVM, Traditional Neural Networks, LDA, CRF.
- **Generative models** model P(x,y) and infer y: Naive Bayes, HMM, Bayesian Networks, LDA (Latent Dirichlet Allocation), Gaussian Mixture Models.
